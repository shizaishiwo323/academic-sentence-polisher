# Scientific Style Principles

## Core target

A strong scientific sentence is clear, concrete, restrained, and easy to verify. It lets the reader identify:

- what object, signal, model, process, or population is discussed;
- what changed, was measured, was compared, or was inferred;
- how strong the evidence is;
- which conditions and sources bound the claim.

## Sentence architecture

- use a short concrete subject when possible;
- bring the main verb early;
- reduce stacked abstract nouns;
- place conditions and long modifiers after the main subject and verb when clarity improves;
- preserve comparison baselines and scope markers;
- split a sentence when observation, interpretation, and implication need separate evidence.

## Verb choice

Prefer direct verbs such as:

- `show`;
- `indicate`;
- `suggest`;
- `measure`;
- `compare`;
- `estimate`;
- `increase`;
- `decrease`;
- `shift`;
- `distinguish`.

Use `demonstrate`, `establish`, `confirm`, `reveal`, `control`, and `ensure` only when evidence supports their strength.

## Results and mechanisms

Prefer:

```text
observation -> comparison -> interpretation -> bounded implication
```

Report the measured change before broad meaning. Add a mechanism only when it is already stated or supported.

## Restraint

- name the concrete contribution;
- use exact numbers instead of decorative intensifiers;
- keep implications within tested conditions;
- avoid new conceptual labels and decorative compounds;
- preserve null and unchanged quantities when they matter.

## Reader guidance

Use topic sentences, controlled repetition, explicit referents, and connectors with real logical functions. Do not force stylistic variety at the cost of technical consistency.
