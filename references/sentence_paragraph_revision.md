# Sentence and Paragraph Revision

## 1. Diagnose the sentence's job

A sentence may:

- establish context;
- report prior research;
- identify a gap;
- state an aim or hypothesis;
- describe a method;
- report a result;
- compare evidence;
- interpret a result;
- qualify a claim;
- state a limitation or implication;
- guide the reader to a figure, table, or equation.

Choose grammar and emphasis that support that job.

## 2. Sentence priorities

### Put actor and action in view

Keep the main subject manageable and the main verb close to it.

Prefer a concrete actor, process, signal, model, or result over a long abstract noun phrase.

### Prefer direct ordinary verbs

Consider:

- `analyzed` instead of `performed an analysis of`;
- `increased` instead of `showed an increase in`;
- `compared` instead of `made a comparison between`;
- `because` instead of `due to the fact that`.

Retain nominalizations when they are established technical concepts.

### Control information load

Split when:

- two claims need different evidence or citations;
- a modifier can attach to more than one phrase;
- the sentence changes from observation to interpretation;
- several conditions delay the main verb excessively;
- the logical relation cannot be made clear in one sentence.

Do not split tightly integrated mathematical or definitional statements merely to shorten them.

### Place modifiers next to their target

Check:

- `only`, `also`, `respectively`, `approximately`, `significantly`;
- temporal, spatial, and instrumental phrases;
- participial phrases;
- figure/table qualifiers;
- `using` and `based on`.

### Preserve parallelism

Coordinate items should use compatible grammatical forms.

### Use explicit logical relations

Choose connectors by meaning, not variety:

- cause: `because`, `as a result of`;
- result: `therefore`, `consequently`;
- contrast: `whereas`, `however`;
- concession: `although`, `nevertheless`;
- example: `for example`;
- sequence: `subsequently`, `after`, `during`.

### Avoid inflated or vague wording

Prefer a concrete contribution or relation to stock phrases such as:

- `groundbreaking`;
- `provides valuable insights`;
- `plays a crucial role`;
- `opens a new avenue`;
- `a comprehensive understanding`.

## 3. Paragraph function

A paragraph should normally have one dominant function, for example:

- synthesize a literature theme;
- define a gap;
- justify a method;
- describe one procedure;
- report one result pattern;
- interpret one mechanism;
- discuss one limitation;
- derive one implication.

## 4. Paragraph sequence

Common defensible sequences:

```text
claim -> evidence -> explanation -> bounded implication
general pattern -> key details -> exception -> synthesis
method choice -> implementation -> quality control -> limitation
prior approach A -> prior approach B -> unresolved issue
observation -> comparison -> interpretation -> consequence
```

## 5. Cohesion

Use:

- controlled repetition of key scientific nouns;
- explicit referents such as `this decrease`, not bare `this`;
- old-to-new information flow;
- connectors that name the actual relation;
- parallel structure for comparison or sequence.

Scientific prose does not require a synonym for every repeated technical noun.

## 6. Paragraph boundaries

Start a new paragraph when:

- the main function changes;
- a new result pattern, mechanism, condition, or scale begins;
- the discourse moves from observation to substantial interpretation;
- a limitation or implication needs independent development.

Avoid both one-sentence fragmentation and paragraphs containing several unrelated jobs.

## 7. Revision method

1. Label each sentence by function.
2. State the paragraph's main job in one line.
3. Identify sentences that do not support it.
4. Reorder by a defensible evidence sequence.
5. Repair only necessary transitions.
6. Revise sentence form after order is stable.
7. Check the ending and link to the next paragraph.

## 8. Verification

- Is the subject and main verb easy to identify?
- Is agency clear?
- Are observation and interpretation distinguishable?
- Are comparison members comparable?
- Are pronoun referents clear?
- Does every citation support the intended claim?
- Are tense and modality intentional?
- Could a modifier attach to the wrong phrase?
- Does the paragraph perform one recognizable function?
