# Grammar, Tense, Voice, Articles, and Cohesion

## 1. Tense as rhetorical meaning

Do not normalize every verb to one tense.

| Function | Common default | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| accepted fact or current generalization | present simple | treated as currently valid |
| active research area with present relevance | present perfect | prior activity remains relevant now |
| specific completed cited study | past simple | bounded past research event |
| standard procedure or equation | present simple | conventional or generally applicable |
| procedure performed in this study | past simple | completed study-specific action |
| result observed in this study | past simple | bounded finding |
| result stated as a general relationship | present simple | stronger/generalized status |
| present paper's action | present simple | `This paper examines...` |

A tense shift should correspond to a change in function.

### Introduction

Typical pattern:

```text
present simple for established context
present perfect for the current state of research or unresolved gap
past simple for named completed studies
present simple for what the paper does
```

### Methods

Distinguish:

- standard procedure: `X is measured using...`
- this study: `X was measured using...`

If the field uses present tense for study-specific procedures, use explicit agency such as `In this study` or `we`.

### Results

Past simple is the conservative default for observations. Present simple may suit:

- what a figure currently shows;
- an equation or mathematical relationship;
- a finding intentionally framed as generally valid.

Do not use present tense merely to make a result stronger.

### Abstract

Tenses may mix:

- background/problem: present simple;
- methods: past simple;
- results: past simple or carefully chosen present simple;
- current contribution/application: present simple or present perfect.

## 2. Active and passive voice

Use active voice when it clarifies:

- who made an analytical decision;
- what the authors contributed;
- which study produced a result.

Use passive voice when:

- process or object is the information focus;
- the actor is obvious and irrelevant;
- journal convention favors it;
- procedural description is clearer.

Avoid agentless passive when it obscures whether work belongs to the current study or a cited study.

Acceptable subjects include `we`, `this study`, `the analysis`, `the model`, `the experiment`, `the results`, and `Figure 2`.

Do not use `we` to mean people in general.

## 3. Articles and determiners

A singular count noun normally needs a determiner:

- `a method`;
- `the method`;
- `this method`;
- `each method`.

Use `a/an` for a newly introduced or non-unique referent. Use `the` when the referent is shared, previously introduced, defined, unique in context, or otherwise identifiable.

The choice may change meaning:

- `a relationship` allows that a relationship may exist;
- `the relationship` presupposes a particular relationship.

Use zero article with many generic plurals and uncountable nouns. Check nouns such as `analysis`, `research`, `evidence`, `equipment`, `behavior`, and `work` for the intended countability.

Use `an` before a vowel sound and `a` before a consonant sound.

## 4. Modifier and adverb placement

Place modifiers close to their targets. High-risk words include:

- `only`;
- `also`;
- `respectively`;
- `approximately`;
- `significantly`;
- `mainly`;
- `initially`;
- `subsequently`;
- `using`;
- `based on`.

If an introductory participial phrase lacks a clear subject, rewrite it. If temporal, spatial, and instrumental modifiers cluster, reorder or split.

## 5. Sequence

Use sequence language to convey the needed detail:

- prior state;
- start;
- next step;
- short delay;
- later stage;
- simultaneous event;
- final stage;
- post-experiment action.

Prefer explicit time or condition information over repeated `then`.

## 6. Frequency and quantity

Frequency terms are interpretive. Use numbers when reproducibility or comparison requires them.

Quantity words frame evidence:

- magnitude: `substantial`, `small`, `negligible`;
- comparison: `higher`, `lower`, `similar`;
- proximity: `approximately`, `nearly`;
- emphasis: `as high as`, `only`;
- neutral quantity: `some`, `several`.

Every evaluative quantity word needs a reference point. `Only 20%` and `as much as 20%` frame the same number differently.

Use `significant` for statistical significance only when that is what is meant; otherwise use a direct number or a supported descriptive word.

## 7. Sentence connection

Close the gap between sentences through:

- controlled lexical overlap;
- a clear pronoun or pro-form;
- a semicolon or relative clause when closely related;
- a connector that names cause, result, contrast, concession, addition, or sequence.

Do not add a connector until the actual relationship is known.
